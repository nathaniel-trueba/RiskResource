# 1. The problem, why it matters, and what Risk Resource does about it

## The problem

If you want to know how exposed your home is to natural disasters, the information exists, but it's scattered across many government agencies. Each one was built for its own specialists:

- **FEMA** keeps decades of disaster declarations and a national model of expected losses.
- **The National Weather Service** issues live warnings, such as Red Flag Warnings for fire weather, flood warnings and high wind advisories.
- **USGS** tracks every earthquake as it happens.
- **NOAA's river gauge network** reports whether rivers near you are rising toward flood stage.
- **Weather models** forecast the heat, dryness and wind that drive wildfire.

None of these sources answers the question a resident actually has: *"How worried should I be about my home, right now?"* Each speaks its own language: percentiles, alert codes, magnitudes, flood categories. Each covers only one hazard, and most separate long-term history from what's happening today. Putting the picture together means visiting several websites and interpreting technical data, usually at the moment when people have the least time to do it.

## Why it matters

- **Disasters are local, and people move.** Someone who moves from the Midwest to California swaps tornado and flood exposure for wildfire and earthquake risk, often without realizing how different preparation looks.
- **History and the present both matter.** A county with a long record of wildfires isn't in equal danger every week. Risk spikes when it's hot, dry and windy. A long-term rating alone misses the weeks that matter most, and a live alert alone arrives with little warning.
- **Wildfire in particular has become faster and more destructive.** Recent fires such as Paradise (2018), Lahaina (2023) and Los Angeles (2025) moved quickly into populated areas. Knowing that a week is unusually dangerous gives people time to prepare an evacuation plan, clear brush and sign up for alerts.
- **Clear information drives action.** People are more likely to prepare when risk is explained in plain terms and tied to their own location.

## How Risk Resource addresses it

Risk Resource brings these sources together into one place, organized around a simple idea: **one clear score for your county, built from parts you can see.**

- **One 0 to 100 threat score per county and state,** with a plain tier: Low, Moderate, Elevated or Critical.
- **Six hazards scored separately:** wildfire, earthquake, flood, severe storm, hurricane and tornado. You can see which ones drive your score.
- **History plus the present.** Every score combines a county's long-term disaster record with live conditions (active warnings, recent earthquakes, forecast winds, river levels), so it changes when conditions change.
- **A forward-looking wildfire forecast.** A machine-learning model estimates, every week, how likely a large wildfire is to start in each county, using recent weather, the forecast and local fire history.
- **An interactive national map.** You can explore by state or county, color the map by overall risk or by a single hazard, and click any area for its breakdown.
- **Search by place.** Type a county, city or ZIP code and go straight to it.
- **Expected losses, kept separate.** FEMA's estimates of average yearly damage (in dollars) are shown alongside the score, so people can see both "how likely" and "how costly".
- **Transparent methodology.** An About page explains every ingredient of the score, with an interactive explainer that shows how each part moves the number.

**In one sentence:** Risk Resource turns scattered federal hazard data into a single, explainable, up-to-date risk score for every US county, with extra focus on wildfire, the hazard where week-to-week conditions matter most.
